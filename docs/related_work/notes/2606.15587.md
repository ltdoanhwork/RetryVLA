## 2. Problem

Fine manipulation (insertion, stacking, alignment). A fluent human teleoperator does the approach fast and the
decisive alignment step in very few frames (pen-cap: about 10 % of frames are alignment, Fig. 1). Imitation loss weights
all frames equally, so the policy learns transport well and alignment poorly. Also, the expert almost never enters a
misaligned state, so the data shows success but not how to come back to it.

## 3. Core idea: what it fundamentally exploits

**Vietnamese summary.** Bài này khai thác *khoảng cách giữa "demo tốt cho người làm" và "demo học được cho policy"*:
expert giỏi nén pha căn chỉnh và sửa sai vào vài frame và gần như không bao giờ đi vào trạng thái lệch, nên dữ liệu
"hoàn hảo" lại thiếu đúng phần policy cần (độ phủ trạng thái lệch + hành vi sửa). Họ tách hai hiệu ứng: (i) *trọng số*
(resample các frame quan trọng) và (ii) *độ phủ trạng thái recovery* (demo "deliberate": người thao tác cố ý tiếp cận
lệch trục rồi sửa). Kết luận chính: phần lớn lợi ích đến từ (ii) độ phủ, không phải (i). Về mặt mô hình, STAIR khai
thác *chuyển động ngắn hạn đã có sẵn trong video demo* (dùng như thông tin "đặc quyền" chỉ có lúc train): encoder video
đóng băng (WanVAE) + projector tạo dynamic token từ các frame lân cận *tương lai*, rồi dạy VLM dự đoán token đó chỉ từ
frame hiện tại, nên lúc deploy không cần video. Nói gọn: nó khai thác (a) độ nhạy không đều theo pha (σ_al ≫ σ_tr) và
(b) tín hiệu động học miễn phí trong trajectory, distill qua kênh train-only, không phải thêm dữ liệu.

In one English line: *the expert's demonstration distribution is optimal for the expert's execution, not for the
learner's learning; the missing ingredient is coverage of off-nominal (recovery) states, and future-frame motion can
be distilled as a train-time-only signal.*

## 4. Method

1. **Formalization (Sec. 3.1, Eq. 1).** `L_BC = (1-α) L_tr + α L_al`, α = alignment-frame fraction. Phase sensitivity
   `σ_φ ∝ |∂P(success)/∂a_t|`, with `σ_al ≫ σ_tr`. Fluent demos create a "duration-sensitivity mismatch".
2. **Deliberate demonstrations (Sec. 3.2).** Same operators, normal transport, but near contact they slow down, make
   small corrections and "occasionally approach with a mild offset before recovering". Matched trajectory count
   (100 / task), longer trajectories.
3. **Critical-segment resampling (Sec. 3.3, Eq. 2).** Upweight frames within radius r of key moments (gripper change,
   contact, insertion) by λ.
4. **STAIR (Sec. 3.4-3.5, Eqs. 3-8, Fig. 2).** Stage I: frozen WanVAE encodes a short future clip `N_t = {o_{t+δ}}`,
   trainable 3D-conv + Q-Former projector → compact dynamic tokens → MLP → cross-attention into a flow-matching DiT
   action expert. Stage II: freeze the dynamic pathway; a Q-Former on VLM hidden states predicts the projected dynamic
   condition from the current frame (cosine loss), action expert keeps training on the frozen target. Inference: current
   RGB + instruction only.

## 5. Key results (v2 numbers)

Real robot: dual-arm AgileX Piper, 6 tasks in 3 difficulty levels, 100 demos / task, **15 trials / task**, single
training run per cell (no seeds or CIs reported).

| Setting | Easy | Mid | Hard | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| π0.5 Fluent (Tab. 1) | 90.0 | 56.7 | 10.0 | 52.2 |
| π0.5 Deliberate | 93.3 | 73.3 | 36.7 | **67.8** |
| π0.5 Fluent + Resample | 90.0 | 66.7 | 20.0 | 58.9 |
| Own VLA Baseline Fluent (Tab. 2) | 90.0 | 40.0 | 3.3 | 44.4 |
| Baseline Deliberate | 93.3 | 56.7 | 20.0 | 56.7 |
| Baseline Fluent + Resample | 90.0 | 46.7 | 10.0 | 48.9 |
| STAIR Fluent | 93.3 | 56.7 | 13.3 | 54.4 |
| STAIR Deliberate | 96.7 | 63.3 | 26.7 | **62.2** |

- The gap grows with difficulty: on Hard, π0.5 goes from 10.0 (fluent) to 36.7 (deliberate), resample only 20.0.
- **Coverage vs weight (Sec. 4.4, Fig. 5).** Resampling is tuned so its effective alignment budget (88 frames per
  trajectory, up from 22) matches deliberate (95). Resampling keeps the narrow fluent state spread in (Δx, Δz);
  deliberate covers more because of off-axis approaches and recoveries. The authors call the remaining gap "a coverage
  effect, not a weight effect".
- SimplerEnv (Tab. 4-5, OXE pretrain → Bridge + Fractal): Google Robot overall 67.2 % (π0-FAST 60.5); WidowX success
  avg 65.6 % (UniVLA 45.6). 30 rollouts per task.

## 6. Limitations (theirs + ours)

Theirs (Sec. 6): the dynamic feature is predicted deterministically, so it cannot represent multi-modal contact
futures; it uses vision only (no force / tactile); key frames and neighbourhood are hand-set hyperparameters.

Ours:
- Small evaluation: 15 trials per task and one run per cell, so one success is 6.7 pt per task. The v1 → v2 revision
  moved every cell by 1-2 successes per task, and the abstract was not updated.
- The coverage claim rests on one qualitative scatter (Fig. 5) plus success rates. There is no state-coverage metric
  and no test with matched coverage and different weights.
- "Recovery" here means *human-designed* off-axis approaches. These are not the learner's own failure states and are
  not selected by the learner's competence. The paper never measures which recovery states the learner actually visits.
- Deliberate trajectories have more frames. The authors match frame budgets only for the resampling comparison.
- The STAIR gain is a representation gain, separate from the data question (it is complementary: STAIR-Deliberate is
  the best cell).

## 7. How it supports RetryVLA

**Framing check first.** The "teacher" here is a fluent *human teleoperator*, not a privileged RL / PPO expert, and the
gap is about *learnability and coverage*, not observation asymmetry. So it does **not** directly support the
privileged-information half of the argument against Kintsugi-VLA. Use the works in Sec. 9 for that. It does support
the **learnability half**: an expert that is good at the task is not automatically a good source of training data for
the learner.

Arguments it strengthens:

1. **Expert competence ≠ learner learnability (the core of p_rec^E vs Δp).** In this paper the expert succeeds almost
   every time and still produces the worst data. This is direct evidence that a recovery signal defined by the expert
   (Kintsugi's p_rec^E) can be far from what helps the target policy (Δp).
   Citable (Abstract, last sentence): *"These results call for a more pedagogical view of robot data, optimized for
   machine learnability rather than human efficiency alone."*
   Citable (Sec. 1, para. 2): *"a demonstration optimized for human execution is not optimized for robot learning."*
2. **Experts do not visit the states the learner needs.** Citable (Sec. 1, para. 2): *"Worse, an expert almost never
   enters a misaligned state, so the data shows the robot what success looks like but rarely how to recover toward
   it."* This is the covariate-shift / coverage reason to generate data *at the student's off-nominal states* (our L1-L4
   perturbations and the student's own failure states). It is also the "Coverage" condition of Walsman et al. (Sec. 9).
3. **New recovery states beat reweighting existing data.** Citable (Sec. 4.4): *"The remaining gap is therefore a
   coverage effect, not a weight effect: once alignment is sufficiently weighted, success is limited by whether the
   data exposes the local correction process, which replaying the same frames cannot create."* Numbers: π0.5 Hard 36.7
   (deliberate) vs 20.0 (resample) vs 10.0 (fluent), with matched alignment budget (95 vs 88 frames / trajectory, Fig. 5).
   This supports *generating* verified recovery segments (our search + sim verification) over reweighting or filtering
   nominal demos (DemInf / Re-Mix style curation). It is also a reason to include a "nominal demos, upweighted near
   failure" baseline.
4. **Smooth expert data is hard to learn from** (links to RELATED_WORK M1 / M3: Agentic-Teacher's raw code-policy data
   gives 0 %, sampling-planner demos hurt BC). This is a third independent failure mode of "expert-quality" data
   (fluent human), next to scripted / planner experts and privileged RL experts.
5. **Train-time-only privileged signal, distilled into a standard-interface student.** STAIR uses future frames that
   are unavailable at deployment and distills them into a predictor on current-frame input. This is the same pattern as
   our WM / critic used only at data-generation time, with the deployed VLA keeping its normal interface. Useful
   analogy for "privileged information belongs in the data pipeline, not in the imitation target".

How to phrase it in our related work (suggested, 2 sentences):
> Liu et al. (2026, arXiv:2606.15587) show that fluent expert demonstrations can be poor teachers for fine
> manipulation: experts rarely enter misaligned states, and adding coverage of recovery states (deliberate demos) helps
> much more than reweighting the critical frames already in the data (π0.5 hard-tier success 10.0 → 36.7 vs 20.0).
> RetryVLA takes this one step further: which recovery states are covered is decided by the *target policy's* own
> failures and competence, not by the demonstrator, and each recovery is checked by the policy's own continuation.

Where it **weakens or overlaps** our claims:
- *Representation alternative.* STAIR on fluent data alone recovers most of the deliberate gain (44.4 → 54.4 vs 56.7,
  v2). A reviewer can ask whether a better representation (or a future / WM auxiliary loss, cf. our H section and
  `L_future`) removes the need for recovery data. We should report that recovery data and a dynamics representation are
  complementary. Their own STAIR-Deliberate (62.2) > STAIR-Fluent (54.4) supports complementarity.
- *Coverage without competence.* Their gain comes from coverage designed by the demonstrator, with no student-aware
  selection. So "add recovery-state coverage" is already shown in a real-robot setting (with DART and RaC before it).
  Our novelty must stay in **student-aware selection** (Δp / p_rec^{π0}) plus ground-truth verification, not in "recovery
  data helps".
- *Their analysis does not separate p_rec^E from learnability.* Deliberate demos are made by the same expert, so the
  paper cannot tell whether the gain comes from the learner's needs or simply from more off-axis states. It supports
  "expert-optimal ≠ learner-optimal" but gives no evidence that *student-competence-based* selection beats uniform
  coverage. We still need our own Δp ablation.

## 9. Further reading to cite (teacher-student mismatch; all IDs opened on arxiv.org/abs, venue from comments or proceedings)

1. **Impossibly Good Experts and How to Follow Them**, Walsman, Zhang, Choudhury, Fox, Farhadi, **ICLR 2023**
   ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=AYPMk-ezt8), [code](https://github.com/aaronwalsman/impossibly-good); no
   arXiv version found). This is the formal version of our argument: an expert with privileged information can be
   "impossibly good" (`V^{π**} > V^{π*_θ}`), and imitation recovers the best agent policy only under **Coverage**
   (training must visit every state the agent-optimal policy visits) and **Correctness** (expert advice must match the
   agent-optimal action) (Theorem 1). Kintsugi's PPO expert breaks Correctness, and expert-chosen start states break
   Coverage. Best single citation for "p_rec^E is not the student's p_rec".
2. **ADVISOR: Bridging the Imitation Gap by Adaptive Insubordination**, Weihs, Jain, … Schwing, **NeurIPS 2021**,
   [arXiv 2007.12173](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173). Names the "imitation gap": a privileged teacher's actions can be
   impossible to infer from the student's observations. The student mixes in RL weighted by how well it can imitate.
3. **Robust Asymmetric Learning in POMDPs (A2D)**, Warrington, Lavington, Ścibior, Schmidt, Wood, **ICML 2021**,
   [arXiv 2012.15566](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15566). Shows that imitating a fully observing expert can give an
   arbitrarily bad partially observing policy, and fixes this by adapting the *expert* to the student's information.
   Supports "the teacher must be student-aware".
4. **TGRL: Teacher Guided Reinforcement Learning**, Shenfeld, Hong, Tamar, Agrawal, **ICML 2023**,
   [arXiv 2307.03186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03186). Weights the teacher-imitation term by whether following the
   teacher actually beats the student's own RL, close to our Δp (learning benefit) criterion.
5. **Leveraging Fully Observable Policies for Learning under Partial Observability**, Nguyen, Baisero, Wang, Amato,
   Platt, **CoRL 2022**, [arXiv 2211.01991](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01991). Robot manipulation, state-based expert
   → image student. Uses the expert as guidance inside RL instead of imitating it directly, because direct imitation
   fails under asymmetry.
6. **Learning by Cheating**, Chen, Zhou, Koltun, Krähenbühl, **CoRL 2019**,
   [arXiv 1912.12294](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12294), and **RMA**, Kumar, Fu, Pathak, Malik, **RSS 2021**,
   [arXiv 2107.04034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034). These are the canonical *successful* privileged-teacher →
   student distillations with on-policy (DAgger-style) relabelling. Cite them as the standard recipe that Kintsugi-style
   pipelines follow, and note that they work when the student can, in principle, infer the teacher's action.

Also useful: **DART** [arXiv 1703.09327](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09327) (CoRL 2017; supervisor-side noise to expose
recoveries, a direct ancestor of "deliberate demos"), **HG-DAgger** [arXiv 1810.02890](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02890)
and **ThriftyDAgger** [arXiv 2109.08273](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08273) (CoRL 2021; data gathered at the
*student's* states, gated by novelty / risk), and **Privileged Sensing Scaffolds RL** (Hu et al., ICLR 2024,
[arXiv 2405.14853](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14853); privileged information used in critic / WM / exploration, not as
the imitation target, which is the same design choice as our WM + critic used only at data-generation time).
